# Customer concentration in a digital business acquisition: how buyers assess it

*By Joash Boyton, CEO of [Acquiry](https://www.acquiry.com/), Melbourne*

Customer concentration is one of the first things an experienced buyer looks for in a digital business, and one of the last things a seller wants to talk about. A business can show strong revenue growth and healthy margins and still carry a risk that one email from one customer could undo. This note sets out how I suggest buyers measure that risk, how it tends to show up in price and terms, and what to ask for before an offer becomes binding.

## What counts as concentration

There is no single threshold that applies to every business, but buyers usually start asking questions when:

- one customer accounts for a large share of revenue or gross profit,
- the top five or ten customers together make up most of the revenue, or
- a single channel, platform or reseller sits between the business and most of its customers.

The last point is often missed. A SaaS product sold mainly through one marketplace, or a content business that relies on one search engine or one affiliate programme, has concentration even if no single end customer is large.

## Measure it properly

Ask for the data rather than the summary. A useful first request is:

1. Revenue by customer, by month, for at least 24 months, exported from the billing or accounting system.
2. Gross profit by customer where delivery costs differ between customers.
3. Contract terms for the largest customers: length, renewal dates, notice periods, pricing reviews and any termination rights on change of control.
4. Revenue by channel or platform, with the commercial terms of each channel.

From that, work out the share of revenue and gross profit held by the largest customer, the top five and the top ten, and how those shares have moved over time. A top customer whose share is falling because the rest of the business is growing is a different risk from one whose share is rising.

## Read the relationship, not just the number

Two businesses with the same concentration figures can carry very different risk. Questions worth asking:

- **How long has the customer been with the business, and why do they stay?** Deep integration, data lock-in or a unique capability is more durable than price.
- **Who owns the relationship?** If it sits with the founder personally, the risk rises at completion.
- **Is there a written contract, and what happens on a change of control?** Some contracts allow termination or renegotiation when the owner changes.
- **Has the customer had pricing changes, disputes or service issues?** A recent discount request can be an early signal.

Where it is appropriate and agreed with the seller, a short, structured conversation with the largest customers late in the process can settle a lot of uncertainty. It should be planned carefully, because it carries confidentiality risk for the seller.

## How concentration shows up in price and terms

Buyers rarely walk away from concentration outright. More often it changes how they value and structure the deal. Common approaches include:

- **A lower multiple** applied to earnings, reflecting the higher chance of a revenue drop. Comparable transaction data helps frame this; see [Acquiry's digital business valuation multiples](https://www.acquiry.com/data/multiples/).
- **Adjusting earnings** to exclude or haircut revenue from a customer whose contract ends soon after completion.
- **Deferred or contingent consideration**, such as an earn-out tied to retention of named customers.
- **Specific warranties and indemnities** about the status of key contracts and any known disputes.
- **A longer transition period** where the seller introduces the buyer to key customers and supports the handover.

Each of these moves risk between the parties in a different way. Sellers tend to accept structure more readily when the reasons are clear and supported by the data.

## Before you sign

Before an offer becomes binding, a buyer should be able to answer three questions in writing: how much revenue and gross profit sits with the largest customers, how secure those relationships are after completion, and how the price and terms reflect any remaining risk. If those answers are not clear, the diligence is not finished.
