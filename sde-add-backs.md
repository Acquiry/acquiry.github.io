# Normalising SDE: the add-backs a digital business buyer should question

*By Joash Boyton, CEO of [Acquiry](https://www.acquiry.com/), Melbourne*

Most smaller digital businesses are priced as a multiple of seller's discretionary earnings (SDE). The multiple gets most of the attention in negotiation, but the earnings figure it is applied to often moves the price just as much. Every dollar added back to profit is a dollar that gets multiplied. This note covers how I suggest buyers review a seller's add-backs before agreeing on the earnings base.

## What SDE is meant to show

SDE starts with net profit and adds back items that a new owner would not expect to carry: the owner's own salary and benefits, interest, depreciation and amortisation, and genuinely one-off costs. The aim is to show the cash a single owner-operator could take from the business in a normal year. If you want to work through the arithmetic yourself, Acquiry's free [SDE and EBITDA calculator](https://www.acquiry.com/tools/sde-ebitda-calculator/) sets out the standard adjustments.

The concept is sound. The trouble is that the word "normal" leaves a lot of room.

## Add-backs that usually hold up

These are generally accepted when they are supported by records:

- **Owner salary, superannuation and benefits**, provided the buyer has also accounted for the cost of replacing the owner's work (see below).
- **Interest and finance costs** on debt that will not transfer to the buyer.
- **Depreciation and amortisation**, although buyers of businesses with real capital needs, such as hardware or content libraries, should look at actual replacement spend instead.
- **Clearly one-off costs** such as a documented legal matter that has closed, or a single office relocation.

## Add-backs that deserve a harder look

These are where most disagreements start:

- **"One-off" costs that recur.** A website rebuild, a migration or a "special" marketing push that appears in two of the last three years is a running cost, not a one-off.
- **Personal expenses run through the business.** Often legitimate to add back, but they should be itemised and traceable, not estimated.
- **Paused marketing or contractor spend.** If revenue growth depended on spend the seller cut in the final year to lift profit, adding it back overstates what the business earns at its current revenue.
- **Unpaid or underpaid family members.** If a relative does real work for little or no pay, a buyer will need to pay market rates for it. That is a deduction, not an add-back.
- **Pro forma savings.** Projected cost cuts the buyer might achieve belong in the buyer's own model, not in the seller's earnings figure.

## Do not forget the replacement cost

Adding back the full owner salary assumes the buyer will do the owner's job for free. For many buyers, particularly investors and strategic acquirers, that is not the plan. A fair normalisation deducts a market cost for the roles the owner fills: general management, sales, product, support or whatever the business actually depends on them for. In founder-led businesses this single adjustment can change the earnings base materially.

## What to ask for

A practical evidence request for add-backs:

1. A schedule listing each add-back by year, with a one-line explanation.
2. The ledger entries or invoices behind each item above an agreed threshold.
3. Profit and loss statements by month for at least 24 months, so recurring patterns are visible.
4. Payroll and contractor records showing who does the work today and at what cost.
5. Marketing spend by month alongside revenue by month.

## Agree the base before the multiple

My suggestion to buyers is simple: agree the earnings base in writing before debating the multiple. Once both sides accept the same normalised figure, a conversation about the multiple can lean on comparable deals; Acquiry publishes [digital business valuation multiples](https://www.acquiry.com/data/multiples/) for that purpose. Arguing both at once tends to produce a number nobody can explain later.
