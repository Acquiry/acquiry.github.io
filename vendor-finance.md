# Vendor finance in a small digital business acquisition: a buyer's view

*By Joash Boyton, CEO of [Acquiry](https://www.acquiry.com/), Melbourne*

Vendor finance, sometimes called seller financing, means the seller agrees to receive part of the purchase price after completion, usually as a loan repaid over an agreed period. It comes up often in smaller digital business deals, where bank lending against websites, software and online stores can be hard to obtain. Used well, it closes a funding gap and keeps the seller interested in a smooth handover. Used carelessly, it creates a second negotiation that runs for years.

## Why it appears

Buyers raise vendor finance for two main reasons. The first is funding: the buyer has most of the price in cash but not all of it. The second is risk sharing: if part of the price is paid over time, the seller has a reason to support the transition and to have described the business accurately.

Sellers sometimes offer it themselves to widen the pool of buyers or to support a higher headline price. A buyer should treat a higher price with deferred payments as exactly that, and compare offers on the value of what is paid and when, not on the headline figure.

## How it differs from an earn-out

Vendor finance and earn-outs both defer part of the price, but they work differently:

- **Vendor finance** is a fixed amount owed. It is repaid on a schedule whether the business performs or not.
- **An earn-out** is contingent. It is paid only if agreed performance targets are met.

Some deals use both. Buyers should be clear about which risk each piece is meant to address.

## Terms to settle in writing

Before signing, a buyer and seller should agree, with their lawyers:

1. **Amount and share of price.** How much is deferred, and what share of the total that represents.
2. **Repayment schedule.** Monthly, quarterly or a lump sum, and the total term.
3. **Interest.** Whether the deferred amount carries interest, and at what rate.
4. **Security.** Whether the seller takes security over the business, its domains, code or other assets, and what happens to that security as the loan is repaid.
5. **Default and remedies.** What counts as a default, any cure period, and what the seller can do if payments stop.
6. **Set-off.** Whether the buyer can reduce repayments to cover warranty claims. Sellers often resist this, so it is worth raising early.
7. **Restrictions on the buyer.** Limits on selling the business, taking on other debt or moving key assets while money is still owed.

## What a buyer should check

Vendor finance does not reduce the need for diligence. If anything, it adds a question: can the business meet its repayments and still be run properly? A buyer should model repayments against conservative cash flow, not the best recent year, and leave room for the costs of replacing the seller's own work. Acquiry's [SDE and EBITDA calculator](https://www.acquiry.com/tools/sde-ebitda-calculator/) is a useful starting point for the earnings side of that model.

It is also worth asking what the seller's willingness, or reluctance, to offer vendor finance signals. A seller who is confident in the business and its handover is usually comfortable with some deferred payment on fair terms.

## Keep it proportionate

Vendor finance works best as one part of a clear deal structure, not as a fix for a price that the business cannot support. If the repayments only work under optimistic assumptions, the better conversation is about the price.
